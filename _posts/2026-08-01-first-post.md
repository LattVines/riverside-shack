# Hello World

## Game Dev

I used to make video games but I sort of had a falling out with it. After making many, trying to improve, and feeling like it was causing me more anguish than joy, I took a step back from it and told myself that I needed to grow creatively in a new direction. One that was not *digital* 1s and 0s. I began to feel like anything on the computer had no value. It was all a counterfeit simulation of life wrapped up in somebody else's algorithm to sell you frosted mini wheats. I was pretty mad that I couldn't even keep my games running on the portals I uploaded on because of policy changes and all the work that goes into maintaining software. It is a lot of thankless work. And it saddened me that the best I could produce was labeled as "shovelware."

I felt bad for playing games too. When you tell people you *make games*, a common reaction you'd hear is one geared toward you being a *gamer*. It felt a bit like they were directing the most negative stereotypes of gamer culture on to me, as an adult, like games were meant for kids and anybody still playing them after age twelve had a compulsive gaming habit. This bothered me. Because I wouldn't classify myself as a compulsive gamer. In fact, if there is a gene for compulsive gambling, I don't have it. Gambling doesn't appeal to me at all, and I have always gone long spans between playing games when I don't feel like it at all, and even resent the time they take to play. I am not compulsive. I deliberate endlessly on everything. Too much. If a game comes out that I want to play, it takes me months to convince myself to buy it. Usually, I convince myself NOT to buy it.

But, I sort of have been a gamer, so judging anybody for it is unfair. I believe people should be allowed to enjoy what they want. I enjoy games. I learned a lot from them and they have given us SO MUCH shared culture. For me, they were often an escape. In times of my life when I felt like I had nobody to talk to, disappearing into a game like the original Baldur's Gate, or countless others, I felt like I wasn't so alone. And I made most of my friends through them, or playing tabletop games like D&D. Trying to install mods for games had to be the genesis of me becoming a programmer. It led to me doing real work for real money. They were one of only a few topics my dad and I were able to talk about.

Games are bad? I don't think so. Anything consumed compulsively can become *bad* but what about television? Staring at a phone? Hustle culture? Or eating crayons?? And hey, I've tried eating a crayon. I did not take to it.

Another example, through a local game dev community, I made my latest small collection of friends. After feeling disillusioned by gamedev, one day, I asked one of them, Taylor, "What is the point of making games?"

"Games are like a warm blanket," she said.

I knew exactly what she meant. I had no response. She was right. I felt bad for allowing myself to become as jaded as I had, though I can't say it helped me figure out the new direction I needed. It did reinforce one thing: I couldn't disown my own work.

This is my personal blog. I don't know what direction it is going in.

The cover photo of this post is a layered photo of the swampy slough behind the cabin on the bank of the river. One layer of the image is the water in summer. Another from the same position in fall when all the plants are brittle, but ready to return in spring. Plant life clogs it all up. Plastic bottles and lost basketballs float in and decide to stay. It is reptile city. The summer nights are loud with frogs. That is my favorite part.
